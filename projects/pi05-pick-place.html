Fine-tuning π₀.5 for pick and place

A π₀.5 vision-language-action model, fine-tuned so the Pioneer humanoid's left arm picks up a cube and places it in a tray in Isaac Sim. I recorded the demonstrations with a leader arm, wrote the training and evaluation scripts, and trained only the action expert on top of a frozen vision-language backbone.

Overview

The WATonomous humanoid team is building a humanoid called Pioneer. The part I worked with is its bimanual arm rig: two arms with six joints each and a two-finger gripper. My task was to get the left arm to do a simple manipulation task from camera images and one text instruction: pick up the cube and place it in the tray.

Training a policy from scratch would need far more data than one person can record. So I fine-tuned π₀.5, a vision-language-action (VLA) model from Physical Intelligence, using the open-source LeRobot implementation and its pi05_base weights. A VLA takes camera images and a text instruction and outputs robot actions.

The work had three parts: recording demonstrations in simulation, writing a training script that fits on the hardware I could get, and checking whether the result had actually learned anything.

How π₀.5 is built

π₀.5 is two transformers that run side by side.

  • The first is PaliGemma, a vision-language model: a SigLIP image encoder feeding a Gemma language model with 2 billion parameters, about 3 billion in total. It reads the camera images and the text prompt. In π₀.5 the arm's joint state is also written into the prompt as text, after each value is sorted into one of 256 bins.
  • The second is the action expert, a smaller Gemma-style transformer with about 300 million parameters. It takes a chunk of 50 future actions with noise added, and predicts how to remove the noise.

At every layer the two share one attention step. The action tokens can look at the image and text tokens, but not the other way round. So the backbone works out what is in the scene and what the instruction means, and the action expert turns that into motion.

Flow matching

Actions come out through flow matching. During training, a clean action chunk from a demonstration is mixed with random noise, and the model learns to predict the direction that leads from the clean actions to the noise. At run time it starts from pure noise and takes 10 small steps back along that direction. What is left is a chunk of 50 actions, two seconds of motion at 25 Hz. In my evaluation the first 10 steps (0.4 s) of each chunk are executed before the policy plans again.

Freezing the backbone

The pretrained backbone already knows how to read images and language. What it has never seen is this arm, these cameras, and this task. With 71 short demonstrations, updating about 3 billion backbone parameters would mostly risk damaging what the model already knows, and it needs far more memory. So I froze the whole vision-language model and trained only the parts that produce actions: the action expert, the two projections that map actions into and out of it, and the small network that encodes the flow-matching time.

Freezing the weights does not save much memory on its own. Because the action expert attends to the backbone at every layer, PyTorch still keeps every backbone activation and traces gradients back through all of it, even though none of those weights will change. But the attention mask already stops the action tokens from influencing the image and text tokens, so the backbone's outputs do not depend on anything being trained. That makes it safe to cut them out of the gradient graph. I wrapped LeRobot's layer function to do that:

_compute_layer_complete = pi05_modeling.compute_layer_complete

def _compute_layer_frozen_prefix(*args, **kwargs):
    prefix_out, suffix_out = _compute_layer_complete(*args, **kwargs)
    return [prefix_out.detach(), suffix_out]

pi05_modeling.compute_layer_complete = _compute_layer_frozen_prefix

After each layer, the backbone's output (the prefix) is detached. Backpropagation then stops at the action expert, and PyTorch no longer has to keep the backbone's intermediate results around for a backward pass that would never use them.

Recording demonstrations

There was no dataset for this arm, so I recorded one in simulation. The Pioneer arm in Isaac Sim is a digital twin of the real one, and I drive it with a physical leader arm: a 3D-printed arm with seven Feetech STS3215 servos, one for each of the six joints and one for the gripper. The servo motors stay off and only their encoders are read. Each leader joint maps directly to the matching joint of the simulated arm, so moving the leader by hand moves the robot the same way, with no inverse kinematics in between.

The recorder I wrote saves four things 25 times a second: the arm's measured joint positions (the state), the joint targets coming from the leader (the action), the camera images, and the text prompt. It writes them as a LeRobot v3.0 dataset. Keys on the keyboard start, save or delete a take.

I also built the scene (a table, a cube and a tray) and set up the three cameras: a base camera looking down at the table, and one on each wrist between the gripper fingers. I set the cameras up to match the real robot's camera specs. The base camera, for example, is a model of the colour camera on an Intel RealSense D455, with that camera's lens. The closer the simulated images are to what the real cameras will see, the less the policy has to relearn when it moves to the real arm.

The finished dataset has 71 episodes and 5,654 frames. Each episode is short, about 3 seconds, because the task is short.

From a recording to model input

The model cannot read a recording directly. LeRobot's pre-processor and post-processor sit between the dataset and π₀.5 and convert in both directions.

  • Images are resized to the 224 × 224 the image encoder expects.
  • The prompt and the binned joint state are turned into tokens.
  • Joint states and actions are normalized. Every dimension has a different range: in this data the wrist joint moves through almost 2 radians, while a gripper finger moves 0.05 metres. π₀.5 rescales each dimension to roughly −1 to 1, using its 1st and 99th percentile in the training data. Without this, the dimensions with the largest numbers would dominate the loss and get the largest gradients.
  • States and actions are padded from 8 values to the 32 the model was built for.
  • On the way out, the post-processor undoes the normalization, so the output is joint targets in radians again.

Delta actions

The dataset stores absolute joint targets. I trained on delta actions instead: each of the 50 steps in a chunk is the offset from where the arm was when the chunk was planned. The gripper stays absolute, because open and closed are targets, not motions. A policy that has not learned much then predicts something close to zero and holds still, instead of drifting toward an average pose.

This conversion cannot be stored in the dataset, because the same frame appears in 50 different chunks, each relative to a different starting state. So the script converts every batch as it trains, and recomputes the normalization statistics for the deltas.

The training script

The training loop is written out by hand instead of calling LeRobot's training command, so every step is visible. From top to bottom:

Setup
Fixes the random seeds, downloads the dataset from the Hugging Face Hub, and checks the Hub and Weights & Biases logins before anything is loaded. A missing token then fails in seconds, not after hours of training.
Model
Loads pi05_base and matches the policy's inputs and outputs to this dataset's cameras and joints. Weights are stored as 32-bit floats and the forward pass runs in bfloat16 under autocast. Half-precision math is faster and smaller, and full-precision weights keep small AdamW updates from being rounded away.
Train and validation split
Shuffles the episodes and holds one out for validation; the other 70 are for training. The split is by episode, not by frame, because neighbouring frames are nearly identical.
Data loading
Each sample is one frame plus the next 50 actions. Batches are shuffled so that one update mixes frames from many episodes, and the last, smaller batch of an epoch is dropped so every update carries the same weight.
Processors
Builds the normalization, tokenization and padding steps from the dataset's statistics, with the action statistics swapped for the delta-action ones.
Freezing
Turns gradients on only for the action expert, the action projections and the time network, and gives the optimizer only those parameters.
Learning rate
AdamW with a peak learning rate of 2.5e-5. It ramps up linearly over the first 100 updates, holds at the peak, then follows a cosine curve down to 10% of the peak over the last 1,000 updates.
Training loop
Each forward pass handles 16 samples. The gradients from two passes are added together before one weight update, so every update is based on 32 samples. Gradients are clipped to a norm of 1.0.
Validation
Every 250 updates the held-out episode is scored with the same noise and flow times each time, so a change in validation loss comes from the weights and not from random sampling.
Checkpoints and logs
Loss, learning rate and gradient norm go to a CSV file and to Weights & Biases. A checkpoint is about 14.5 GB, so the script deletes the old one before writing the new one, then uploads the final one to the Hub.

Making it fit

My laptop's GPU has 12 GB of memory, and π₀.5 ran out of memory on it. I first shrank the batch to 2 samples and accumulated gradients over 16 passes, which still gives 32 samples per update, just slowly. Then I moved the training to a 48 GB cloud GPU. There a batch of 16 with 2 accumulation steps gives the same 32 samples per update in far fewer passes. Gradient accumulation works in both directions: it lets a small GPU imitate a large batch, and it lets me keep the update size fixed while the batch size changes with the hardware.

Three other things keep memory down: the frozen backbone needs no gradients and no optimizer state, the detach above removes the backbone from the backward pass, and autocast does the math in bfloat16.

The first updates of a fine-tune are the riskiest. The gradient norm starts near 3 and falls to about 0.4 within the first 60 updates. A full-size learning rate during that stretch could wreck the pretrained weights, so the warmup keeps those steps small. The cosine decay at the end lets the weights settle.

The first run was underfit

The first cloud run used 1,000 weight updates. At 32 samples per update that is 32,000 samples, or about 5.7 passes over the roughly 5,600 training frames. The loss went down, but a falling loss says little about whether the arm would do the task.

So I wrote an offline evaluation. It feeds recorded camera frames and joint states to the policy, takes the 50-step chunk the policy predicts, and compares it with what I actually did on the leader arm at that moment. It also scores a baseline that does nothing: it keeps commanding the arm's current position. A policy that has learned the task should beat that easily.

It did not. Over the 0.4 seconds that get executed, the policy's average error on the arm joints was about 10 degrees on episodes it had trained on. Holding still was off by about 5.5 degrees. Across all joints and the gripper, the policy's error was 1.67 times the baseline's. A policy that is worse than standing still on its own training data has not learned the demonstrations yet. That is underfitting, and the fix is more training.

Training longer

I raised the run to 6,000 updates, about 34 passes over the data, with the learning rate at its peak for most of it. The training loss fell from about 0.70 in the first updates to about 0.013 in the last. In simulation the arm now picks up the cube and drops it in the tray, as in the video at the top.

The validation loss tells a second story. On the held-out episode it was lowest between about 750 and 2,000 updates, around 0.07, and then climbed to 0.144 by the end while the training loss kept falling. A gap like that is the usual sign of overfitting: past roughly 2,000 updates the model is fitting the 70 training episodes more closely than it generalizes. One held-out episode is a noisy measure, and a loss on predicted action chunks is not the same thing as task success. But the trend is steady. The first run trained too little, this one probably too much, and the best checkpoint for this dataset is likely somewhere in between.

What I would change next

Save checkpoints more often and choose one by validation loss and by success in simulation, not just the last one. Record more demonstrations with more variety in where the cube and tray start. Measure a success rate over many resets instead of judging from one clip. Then try the policy on the real arm.

What I learned

A loss curve is not an evaluation. The first run had a falling loss and a policy that was worse than doing nothing. Comparing against a hold-still baseline made that obvious in one chart.

Freeze what already works. With 71 demonstrations there is not enough data to improve a 3-billion-parameter backbone, only to damage it. Training the action expert alone was cheaper, and it was enough for this task.

Memory is a design constraint. Batch size, gradient accumulation, precision, and what stays in the gradient graph all had to be chosen around the GPU. Knowing why the backbone's activations were being kept, and that it was safe to detach them, mattered more than a bigger machine.
